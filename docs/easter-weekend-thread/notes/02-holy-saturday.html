The forgotten day

Holy Saturday doesn't get much attention. Good Friday has services. Easter Sunday has sunrise and celebration. But Saturday? It's mostly a gap in the story—a day that gets skipped over on the way to resurrection.

Which might be exactly why it's worth paying attention to.

What was happening

The disciples had scattered. When Jesus was arrested in Gethsemane, Mark 14:50 says simply: "Everyone deserted him and fled." Peter followed at a distance, then denied knowing Jesus three times before the rooster crowed. Luke 22:61 says Jesus turned and looked at Peter after the third denial. That look is hard to shake.

By Saturday, they were behind locked doors in fear. John 20:19 mentions this detail—though John places the locked doors on Sunday evening, the fear behind them started Friday night and didn't lift until they had seen something impossible.

The women had watched where Jesus was buried, Mark 15:47. They prepared spices to properly anoint the body—but the Sabbath meant no work. Luke 23:56. So they waited. The grief had nowhere to go.

Matthew adds a detail the other Gospels leave out: the chief priests asked Pilate to seal the tomb and post a guard. They were worried someone would steal the body and claim a resurrection. Matthew 27:62-66. The stone was sealed. The guards took their post. It was very, very over.

A reading for the waiting

The Psalms have always been the language of honest grief. Psalm 22 opens with the line Jesus quoted from the cross—"My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"—and moves through abandonment, physical suffering, and isolation before arriving at trust. It's a long psalm, but Psalm 22:1-21 covers the full arc of that movement from desolation toward something that holds.

Lamentations 3:1-26 works similarly. It's one of the most honest pieces of grief writing in the Bible—no quick resolution, no forced hope. And then, at Lamentations 3:22-23, something shifts: "Because of the Lord's great love we are not consumed, for his compassions never fail. They are new every morning." It doesn't erase the grief. It arrives slowly, after sitting with all of it.

What this day asks of us

Holy Saturday is an invitation to stay in the not-yet. You don't have to rush to resolution. The disciples didn't know Sunday was coming. They just sat with what had happened.

If you're carrying something unresolved—a grief, a question, a long wait for something that hasn't arrived—this is a day that honors that. You're not doing it wrong by not having answers.

One small thing: write one honest sentence about what you're waiting for. You don't have to share it with anyone. It's just a way of naming it before tomorrow.
