Early, while it was still dark

The accounts don't all agree on exactly who went first or precisely what they saw. That's actually one of the things historians find interesting—if someone had invented this story, they probably would have made the details line up more neatly. Instead, you get four different accounts, each with its own angle, that agree on the core: the tomb was empty. And then people started seeing Jesus.

The accounts

Paul's letter to the Corinthians was written around 20 years after the crucifixion and contains what scholars consider the earliest written record of resurrection appearances. In 1 Corinthians 15:3-8, Paul lists witnesses: Peter, the twelve, more than five hundred people at once, James, all the apostles, and then Paul himself. He writes this as if the witnesses are still available to be questioned—some of those five hundred were still alive when he wrote it.

The Gospels focus more on the morning at the tomb. Mark ends abruptly at Mark 16:8—the women find the empty tomb and run away, saying nothing to anyone out of fear. It's a strange, almost unsatisfying ending, which has led scholars to debate for centuries whether the original ending was lost.

Luke 24:13-35 tells the story of two disciples walking to Emmaus on Sunday afternoon who don't recognize Jesus as he walks alongside them. He explains the scriptures. They invite him to stay for dinner. He breaks bread—and they recognize him. Then he vanishes.

But the most detailed and personal account is in John 20:1-18. Mary Magdalene comes to the tomb while it's still dark. She sees the stone moved, assumes the body was taken, and runs to tell the disciples. Peter and the other disciple come to check—they see the burial cloths folded and go home. Mary stays, weeping. Then Jesus speaks to her. She mistakes him for the gardener until he says her name.

What "resurrection" meant

First-century Jewish people had varying beliefs about what would happen after death—many held that there would be a future resurrection of the dead, not simply a spirit living on, but a real bodily existence in a renewed world. The claim of Easter isn't resuscitation, someone revived but still mortal. Paul tries to describe the difference in 1 Corinthians 15:35-44: a transformed existence, the way a plant differs from the seed it came from.

That's part of why the resurrection accounts are strange. People recognize Jesus and don't recognize him. He eats fish, Luke 24:42-43. He appears in a room with locked doors, John 20:19. The disciples touch him, John 20:27. The gospel writers seem to be trying to say: this is real, and it's also unlike anything we have words for.

A reading for today

Read John 20:1-18 if you read nothing else this weekend. It's the most intimate of the resurrection accounts—two confused disciples, a grieving woman, a garden, and a single word: Mary.

Something to carry forward

You don't have to have Easter figured out today. The disciples didn't, even with an empty tomb in front of them—John 20:9 notes they still didn't understand the scripture about resurrection.

Pick one verse that caught your attention over these three days. Write it somewhere—a note, a text to yourself, a corner of a journal. Let it be the one thing you return to this week, not everything at once. That's a good way to end a weekend like this.
