Manuscripts and the New Testament text

We don’t have the original letters and Gospels on papyrus from the first century; we have copies—many of them. Textual critics compare manuscripts to reconstruct the most likely reading when copies disagree. That discipline is how we read Romans, the Gospels, and 1 Corinthians with clarity today.

What this does: It helps us work with a stable New Testament text with high confidence for most passages. What it doesn’t do: It doesn’t settle the resurrection by itself—it’s about what the documents say, not whether every claim in them is true for you.

Christians have long cared about faithful copying; warnings about adding or taking away in Revelation 22:18–19 reflect how seriously the early church treated sacred text, even though that verse applies to Revelation’s own scroll in context.

To see why manuscripts matter and how images are collected, browse the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts (CSNTM).

Standard introductions—pick one with your group or leader: Bruce Metzger’s The Text of the New Testament or Bart Ehrman’s The New Testament: A Historical Introduction to the Early Christian Writings. They disagree on some conclusions; both explain the methods clearly.
